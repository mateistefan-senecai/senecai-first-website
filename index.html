Regulation (EU) 2024/1689

EU AI Act

What it is. The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) is the world's first comprehensive AI law. It classifies AI systems by risk: banned practices (like social scoring or manipulative subliminal techniques), high-risk systems used in areas such as employment, credit scoring, law enforcement, education and critical infrastructure, limited-risk systems that trigger transparency duties (chatbots, deepfakes, emotion-recognition tools), and minimal-risk systems with no specific obligations at all. It also directly regulates general-purpose AI (GPAI) models, with extra duties for the small number of models classified as posing "systemic risk". Its obligations phase in gradually through 2025 and 2027, so the compliance bar keeps moving even for systems already on the market.

Your obligations. What applies to you depends on your role and your system's risk tier. As a provider of a high-risk system, you need a risk management system, data governance controls, technical documentation, automatic logging, a human-oversight design, accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity testing, a conformity assessment, CE marking and registration in the EU database before you can place it on the market. As a deployer, you're responsible for human oversight during actual use, monitoring the system's operation, and — for certain public-sector or high-stakes uses — a fundamental-rights impact assessment. If your system only needs to disclose that a user is interacting with AI, or that content is AI-generated, you sit in the transparency tier instead. If you provide a GPAI model, you owe technical documentation, a training-data summary and a copyright policy, with materially heavier duties if your model crosses the systemic-risk threshold.

How we help. We build and maintain your AI system inventory across everything you've built, bought or quietly embedded through a vendor, determine your legal role and risk classification for each one, and put together the technical documentation, conformity assessment and human-oversight design your tier requires. We track the AI Act's staggered deadlines for you — prohibited practices, GPAI obligations, and high-risk requirements — so a compliance gap never catches you by surprise months after the fact.

Regulation (EU) 2016/679

GDPR

What it is. GDPR (Regulation (EU) 2016/679) is the EU's general data protection law, governing any processing of personal data — including the data used to train, fine-tune, run or improve AI systems. It applies to you regardless of where your company is based, as long as you process the personal data of people in the EU. It rests on principles like lawfulness, purpose limitation, data minimisation, storage limitation and accountability, and it grants individuals enforceable rights over their own data, including access, correction, erasure, portability and the right to object to automated decision-making.

Your obligations. You need a valid legal basis for every processing activity — consent, contract, legitimate interest, legal obligation, and so on — documented in a Records of Processing Activities (ROPA) under Article 30. Higher-risk processing, which covers many AI use cases (large-scale profiling, special-category data, systematic monitoring, automated decision-making with legal effect), requires a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA) completed before you start, not after. You must honour data-subject rights within one month, secure personal data with appropriate technical and organisational measures, notify your supervisory authority of qualifying breaches within 72 hours, and have compliant Data Processing Agreements with every vendor and sub-processor that touches personal data on your behalf. You may also need to appoint a Data Protection Officer if profiling or special-category processing happens at scale, and put Standard Contractual Clauses in place for any transfer of personal data outside the EU/EEA — a common gap for teams using US-based AI or cloud providers.

How we help. We map your data flows end to end, review the legal basis behind every processing activity — training data included — and run DPIAs wherever they're required. We put privacy-by-design controls in place across your product rather than bolting them on afterward, review your vendor and data-processing agreements so your AI and cloud supply chain doesn't quietly become your biggest GDPR exposure, and handle the practical mechanics of international transfers so using a non-EU AI vendor doesn't break your compliance by accident.

Directive (EU) 2022/2555

NIS2

What it is. The NIS2 Directive (EU) 2022/2555 updates the EU's cybersecurity rules, replacing the original NIS Directive with far broader scope and real enforcement teeth. It covers "essential" and "important" entities across 18 sectors — energy, transport, banking, health, digital infrastructure, ICT service management, public administration, manufacturing, and more — pulling in a large number of mid-sized companies that were never directly regulated for cybersecurity before. Each EU member state transposes NIS2 into national law, so exact scope, thresholds and deadlines vary slightly from one country to another.

Your obligations. If you're in scope, you need a documented cybersecurity risk-management framework covering incident handling, business continuity and disaster recovery, supply-chain security, access control, cryptography and vulnerability management. You must report significant incidents in stages: an early warning within 24 hours of becoming aware of it, a fuller incident notification within 72 hours, and a final report within a month. Management bodies must formally approve the cybersecurity risk-management measures and can be held personally liable for non-compliance — in several member states, including a temporary ban from management functions. You'll also need an asset inventory, a policy on the use of cryptography, and — depending on your sector — sector-specific technical rules the European Commission has issued on top of the core directive, plus the same requirements flowed down to your own critical suppliers.

How we help. We start with a scoping and gap assessment to confirm whether — and precisely how — NIS2 applies to you, then build the governance structure, incident-response process and technical controls it requires, including the internal reporting chain that gets an incident to the right people, and to your regulator, well inside the 24-hour and 72-hour windows. Where it makes sense, we help you get ISO 27001-certified, since it covers most of NIS2's technical requirements and gives you an internationally recognised way to demonstrate compliance to regulators, enterprise customers and partners alike.

Regulation (EU) 2022/2554

DORA

What it is. The Digital Operational Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2554) is the EU's ICT risk framework for the financial sector. It applies directly to banks, insurers, investment firms, payment institutions, crypto-asset service providers and most other regulated financial entities — and, just as importantly, to their "critical" ICT third-party providers, a category that increasingly includes cloud platforms, core banking software vendors and AI vendors serving financial-sector clients. DORA has applied directly across the EU since 17 January 2025, without needing separate national transposition, which is part of why many financial entities and their vendors were caught unprepared.

Your obligations. You need a documented ICT risk-management framework covering your systems, protocols and tools, with clear governance and board-level accountability for digital risk — your board is personally accountable for approving the ICT risk strategy and staying informed of major incidents and audit findings. You must classify and report major ICT-related incidents to your regulator on a strict timeline, and run a digital operational resilience testing programme, including — for larger entities — threat-led penetration testing (TLPT) at least every three years, simulating real adversary techniques against live production systems and critical third parties together. You also need a register of all your ICT third-party arrangements, with contractual clauses covering audit rights, data location, exit strategies and permitted sub-outsourcing, plus an assessment of concentration risk from relying too heavily on any single provider.

How we help. We build the ICT risk-management framework, incident classification scheme and regulator-reporting process DORA requires, design and run resilience testing programmes matched to your size and criticality, help you prepare for and coordinate TLPT exercises where they apply, and get your third-party risk register and vendor contracts in shape — including reviewing your AI and cloud vendors' contracts for the specific clauses DORA now demands.

Regulation (EU) 2024/2847

Cyber Resilience Act (CRA)

What it is. The Cyber Resilience Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/2847) sets mandatory cybersecurity requirements for "products with digital elements" sold in the EU — essentially any hardware or software product that connects to a network or another device, from IoT devices and industrial equipment to standalone software and the software components bundled into a larger product. It's the product-security counterpart to NIS2's organisational focus, and it follows a similar CE-marking, conformity-assessment model to the AI Act.

Your obligations. You need to build security in from the design stage: secure-by-default configuration, no known exploitable vulnerabilities at release, encrypted data handling by default, and a documented mechanism to deliver security updates throughout the product's life. You must maintain a vulnerability-handling process for the product's expected lifetime — a minimum of five years — including a coordinated disclosure policy and, for higher-criticality products, a Software Bill of Materials (SBOM). Actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents must be reported to ENISA within 24 hours of becoming aware of them, with a follow-up report at 72 hours and a final report on resolution. Products the CRA treats as higher risk — including certain network and identity-management software — sit in "important" or "critical" classes under Annex III and face stricter conformity requirements than the default self-assessment route, including mandatory third-party assessment.

How we help. We assess whether your product falls in scope and which risk class it sits in, build the secure-by-design and vulnerability-handling processes the CRA requires, put together your technical documentation and SBOM, get your incident-reporting workflow ready before the reporting obligations become enforceable on you, and help you set up the ongoing monitoring and patch-release process regulators and customers will expect for as long as you keep the product on the market.

ISO/IEC 27001:2022

ISO 27001

What it is. ISO/IEC 27001 is the leading international standard for an Information Security Management System (ISMS) — a structured, risk-based way of managing information security across people, process and technology. It isn't an EU regulation, but it has become the de facto way to demonstrate security maturity to regulators, enterprise customers and investors, and it maps cleanly onto much of what NIS2 and DORA already require you to do technically. It's often the single artifact that unlocks an enterprise deal or clears a vendor security questionnaire without a lengthy back-and-forth.

Your obligations. Certification is never legally mandatory, but once you commit to it, you need to run a formal risk assessment and treatment process, implement the Annex A controls that match your risk profile — access control, cryptography, physical security, supplier relationships, secure development and incident management among them — maintain a Statement of Applicability justifying every inclusion and exclusion, and put internal audits and management reviews in place on a recurring cycle. Scope typically stretches across your whole organisation or a clearly defined subset of it, and once certified you're subject to annual surveillance audits and a full recertification audit every three years, run by an accredited external certification body.

How we help. We run the gap assessment against ISO 27001's requirements, help you design and implement the ISMS and the specific Annex A controls that fit your business, prepare your documentation and Statement of Applicability, and get you audit-ready — coordinating directly with the certification body through to certification, and supporting you through the surveillance audits that follow so certification holds up year after year, not just on launch day. For clients also tackling NIS2, we build one ISMS that covers both, instead of doing the same work twice.

ISO/IEC 42001:2023

ISO 42001

What it is. ISO/IEC 42001 is the first international standard for an AI Management System (AIMS) — a structured framework for governing how you design, develop, deploy and monitor AI systems responsibly across their entire lifecycle. Like ISO 27001 for security, it's voluntary, but it's rapidly becoming the standard way to prove AI governance maturity to regulators, enterprise customers and investors. It sits alongside — not instead of — sector rules like the AI Act, and it increasingly appears as a requirement in enterprise AI-vendor security questionnaires, well before most companies have even started thinking about it.

Your obligations. If you pursue certification, you need documented AI policies and objectives, a risk-assessment process specific to AI — covering bias, robustness, data quality and human oversight — defined roles and responsibilities for AI governance, impact assessments for your AI systems, and supplier management for third-party AI components and training data. Scope covers the full AI lifecycle, from data acquisition and design through development, verification, deployment and monitoring, and — as with other ISO management-system standards — once certified you face annual surveillance audits and a recertification cycle to keep it.

How we help. We build your AI governance framework from the ground up — policies, risk-assessment process, impact assessments and supplier controls — aligned to ISO 42001's requirements, and prepare you for certification. Since we're typically already running your AI Act inventory and classification work in parallel, your AI governance and your AI Act compliance reinforce each other instead of duplicating effort, and we map your AIMS controls against the AI Act's own requirements article by article so you can show regulators and enterprise customers exactly where the two overlap.

EU regulations, in plain terms
What is the EU AI Act, and does it apply to us?

The AI Act is the EU's risk-based framework regulating the development, deployment and use of AI systems. Whether — and how — it applies depends on your role (provider, deployer, distributor or importer) and your system's risk classification. Most companies using AI in the EU market carry some obligation, even if it's only transparency requirements. We run inventory and classification exercises to tell you exactly where you stand.

Does GDPR still apply if we're building AI products?

Yes — training data, inference logs and any personal data processed by an AI system are still subject to GDPR. AI doesn't create a carve-out; it usually adds obligations, like DPIAs for higher-risk processing or a clearer legal basis for the data you train on.

What is NIS2 and who does it apply to?

NIS2 is the EU's updated cybersecurity directive. It extends governance and incident-reporting obligations to a much wider set of "essential" and "important" entities than before, and puts personal liability on management for non-compliance. Whether you're in scope is usually the first question we answer in a NIS2 gap assessment.

What is DORA, and does it only apply to banks?

DORA (the Digital Operational Resilience Act) applies to financial entities and their critical ICT providers — so it reaches far beyond banks, into any vendor providing critical ICT services to the financial sector. It requires a documented ICT risk management framework, incident reporting and resilience testing, not just a security policy on paper.

What is the Cyber Resilience Act (CRA)?

The CRA sets cybersecurity requirements for products with digital elements sold in the EU — hardware and software alike — covering secure-by-design development, vulnerability handling and incident reporting throughout the product's lifecycle.

What happens if we don't comply?

Penalties vary by regulation: the AI Act allows fines up to €35M or 7% of global annual turnover, GDPR up to €20M or 4%, and NIS2 puts personal liability on management. Beyond fines, non-compliance increasingly shows up in investor due diligence and enterprise procurement — it's a commercial risk, not just a legal one.

How do I know which regulations actually apply to my business?

That depends on your products, business model, and how you use data and AI — there's no one-size-fits-all answer. Try the compliance checker above, or book a free intro call and we'll map your exposure in a Discover session.
